CHAPTER 01

Foundations

1.1 Supervised Learning

Learn from examples where the answer is already known.

You show the model many inputs paired with their correct answers (labels). It learns the pattern, then predicts answers for new, unseen inputs. "Supervised" = there's a teacher (the labels) guiding it.

ExampleShow a child fruits and name each one — round+red = "apple", long+yellow = "banana". Later they see a new apple and say "apple!" They learned from labeled examples.

Two flavors: classification (predict a category, e.g. spam / not-spam) and regression (predict a number, e.g. house price).

→Needs lots of labeled data, and quality of labels = quality of model. Garbage labels in, garbage predictions out.

1.2 Classification vs Regression

Same idea, different kind of answer: a category vs a number.

Classification picks a bucket ("which class?"). Regression predicts a quantity ("how much?").

ClassificationRegression
OutputCategoryNumber
Example"Spam" / "Not spam"$452,000
MetricAccuracy, F1MAE, RMSE
?Quick test: would averaging two answers make sense? Avg of "cat" & "dog" = nonsense → classification. Avg of $300k & $400k = $350k → regression.

1.3 Unsupervised Learning

No labels — the model finds hidden patterns on its own.

You give raw data with no answers, and the model discovers structure by itself. Main jobs: clustering (group similar things), association (things that go together), and dimensionality reduction (simplify without losing the essence).

ExampleHand a child a box of mixed toys and say nothing. They naturally group cars together, soft toys together — grouping by similarity, with no one telling them the categories.
→Hard to evaluate — there's no "right answer" to check against. Judging if patterns are useful takes human judgment.

1.4 Reinforcement Learning

Learn by trial and error, guided by rewards and penalties.

An agent takes actions in an environment, gets rewards or penalties, and learns the strategy that maximizes reward over time.

ExampleTraining a dog: it sits → treat (reward); it jumps on the couch → "No!" (penalty). Over many tries it learns "sitting = treats."

Key tension: exploration vs exploitation — try new things that might be better, or stick with what already works? (Like picking your usual restaurant vs a new one.)

!The agent optimizes what you reward, not what you intend. Design rewards carefully or it "cheats." RLHF is how models like ChatGPT are polished.

1.5 Feature Engineering

Reshaping raw data into better clues for the model.

A model is only as smart as the clues (features) you feed it. Feature engineering = crafting better ones: creating new features, encoding categories as numbers, scaling, handling missing data, extracting signals from dates/text.

ExampleFor delivery-time prediction, raw addresses are weak. Engineered features like distance_km, is_peak_hour, and is_raining are far more useful.
!Beware feature leakage — accidentally including a feature that gives away the answer (e.g. cancellation_date when predicting cancellations). Looks perfect in testing, useless in reality.

1.6 Model Evaluation Metrics

Scorecards that tell you if a model is actually any good.

The wrong metric makes a bad model look great. Classification: accuracy (% right), but dangerous on imbalanced data. Regression: MAE (average miss), RMSE (punishes big misses), R² ("better than guessing the average?").

The accuracy trapA rare-disease model that always says "healthy" is 99% accurate — but catches zero sick people. Useless, yet "99% accurate."
→Match the metric to the real-world cost of mistakes. Never evaluate on training data — always on unseen data.

1.7 Precision, Recall & F1

Three questions about the two kinds of mistakes.
  • Precision — of what I flagged positive, how much was right? (Punishes false alarms.)
  • Recall — of all real positives, how many did I catch? (Punishes misses.)
  • F1 — one balanced score (harmonic mean); stays low unless both are good.
Which to prioritize?Cancer screening → recall (never miss a case). Spam filter → precision (don't flag real emails).
↔They trade off: flag more → higher recall, lower precision. Choose based on which mistake costs more.

1.8 Overfitting & Regularization

Memorizing vs learning — and how to force real learning.

Overfitting = the model memorizes the training data (including its noise) instead of learning the general pattern. Sign: great on training data, bad on new data.

ExampleA student who memorizes the 50 practice questions aces the practice test but fails the real exam. They memorized, didn't understand.

Regularization = techniques that penalize complexity to keep the model simple and general: L1/L2 (penalize large weights), dropout (randomly switch off neurons), early stopping, and more data.

→You can only detect overfitting by testing on unseen data — which is why we split into train / validation / test sets.

1.9 Hyperparameter Tuning

Finding the best "settings" you choose before training.

Parameters are learned by the model; hyperparameters are set by you beforehand (learning rate, number of layers, tree depth). Tuning = experimenting to find the best combination.

ExampleBaking a cake — oven temperature and baking time are settings you choose before baking. Get them right → perfect cake.

Methods: grid search (try every combo), random search (sample combos — often faster), Bayesian optimization (learn from past attempts).

!Tune on a validation set, never the test set — otherwise you're peeking at the exam and your final score is a lie.
CHAPTER 02

Deep Learning

2.1 Deep Learning

Neural networks with many layers that learn features by themselves.

A powerful subset of ML (AI ⊃ ML ⊃ Deep Learning). "Deep" = many stacked layers. Its superpower: it learns features automatically from raw data instead of you hand-crafting them.

ExampleTo recognize a cat: traditional ML needs you to define "detect whiskers, ears." Deep learning figures out "cat-ness" itself from thousands of photos.

Each layer learns something more abstract: edges → shapes → parts → whole face (like building with LEGO).

→Data-hungry, compute-heavy, and a "black box." For small/tabular data, simpler models often win. Use DL when data is unstructured and abundant.

2.2 Do We Clean Data for Deep Learning?

Yes — DL removes manual feature engineering, not data cleaning.

Deep learning learns features itself, but you still must clean: remove corrupted files, fix mislabeled data, drop duplicates, handle missing values, and — almost always — scale/normalize inputs (e.g. pixels 0–1).

!Garbage in, garbage out never dies. A powerful model on messy data just learns the mess confidently. Teams spend ~70–80% of time on data.

2.3 Neural Networks

Layers of simple "neurons" that learn by adjusting connection strengths.

Each neuron takes inputs, multiplies each by a weight (importance), sums them, and passes the result through an activation function that decides how strongly to "fire." Stack thousands into layers → complex learning.

ExampleDeciding to go to a party: inputs = "is a friend going?", "is it far?"; weights = how much each matters. Sum crosses a threshold → yes/no.

Learns via backpropagation + gradient descent: guess → measure error → adjust weights to reduce it → repeat (like adjusting a shower's temperature knob).

→Weights = the parameters it learns. Activation functions add non-linearity so it can learn curvy patterns, not just straight lines.

2.4 ReLU

Rectified Linear Unit — the most popular activation function.

One simple rule: keep positive inputs as-is, turn negatives into zero. ReLU(x) = max(0, x). Input 5 → 5; input −3 → 0.

AnalogyA one-way valve — lets positive signals through, blocks negatives to zero.
→Simple, fast, and avoids the "vanishing gradient" problem. Catch: "dying ReLU" (stuck at zero) — fixed by variants like Leaky ReLU.

2.5 CNN — Convolutional Neural Network

The network that "sees" — powers image recognition.

Slides a small filter across an image (convolution), detecting patterns wherever they appear — reusing the same filter everywhere (far fewer weights than a regular net). Pooling layers shrink the image while keeping key info.

AnalogyScanning a photo with a magnifying glass, section by section: first edges, then shapes, then parts (eyes, wheels), then whole objects.

Uses: medical scans, face unlock, self-driving vision, defect detection.

→Recognizes a cat wherever it sits in the photo (translation invariance). Needs lots of labeled images; helped hugely by data augmentation.

2.6 RNN — Recurrent Neural Network

For sequences, where order matters — has a "memory."

Processes data one step at a time, carrying a hidden state (memory) forward so context builds up. Good for text, speech, time-series.

AnalogyReading a sentence — you carry meaning forward word by word. "The clouds are in the ___" → "sky", because you remember earlier words.

Problem: plain RNNs forget long-ago context (vanishing gradient). Fixed by LSTM / GRU, which use gates to remember important things and forget the rest.

→Slow (step-by-step) and still struggle with very long context — which is why Transformers replaced them.

2.7 Transformers

The architecture behind ChatGPT, Claude, and modern AI.

Solved RNNs' two flaws: it processes all words at once (fast, parallel) and uses attention to directly connect any word to any other (no forgetting). Introduced in 2017's "Attention Is All You Need."

Example"The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired." Attention lets "it" focus on "animal" to resolve the meaning.

Scales beautifully — more data + parameters + compute = reliably better. This scaling is why AI exploded.

!Attention cost grows quadratically with input length (2× text = 4× work) — the main limit on context window size.

2.8 Encoder / Decoder

Two halves of a Transformer: understanding vs generating.
  • Encoder-only (BERT) — understands input. For search, classification. (A literary critic.)
  • Decoder-only (GPT, Claude) — generates text one word at a time. Today's LLMs. (A storyteller.)
  • Encoder-Decoder (T5) — understands then generates. For translation. (A translator.)
→Modern LLMs are decoder-only — a big enough decoder can do everything (translate, summarize, classify) via next-word prediction.

2.9 Attention Mechanism

Each word decides which other words to focus on.

For every word, the model creates a Query ("what am I looking for?"), matches it against every word's Key ("what do I offer?"), and pulls in the Values of the best matches — building context-aware meaning.

AnalogySearching YouTube: your search = Query, video titles = Keys, video content = Values. Best matches get the most attention.

Multi-head attention does this many times in parallel, each head catching a different relationship (grammar, meaning, etc.).

→No distance limit (word 1 can attend to word 1000), computed in parallel — the core breakthrough of modern AI.

2.10 PyTorch vs TensorFlow

The two main toolkits for building neural networks.
PyTorch (Meta)TensorFlow (Google)
FeelPythonic, easy to debugMature production tooling
Best forResearch, new projects, gen-AILegacy stacks, mobile/edge at scale

→For anything new — especially LLM/gen-AI — PyTorch is the rational default. The concepts transfer; the framework is just syntax.
CHAPTER 03

Large Language Models

3.1 LLMs

Giant Transformers trained on huge text to predict the next word.

At its core, an LLM does one thing: predict the next token, over and over. Scale that up massively and reasoning, writing, and conversation emerge.

AnalogySomeone who read the entire internet and got extraordinarily good at predicting how any sentence should continue.

Built in 3 stages: pre-training (read everything) → fine-tuning (learn to follow instructions) → RLHF (align to human preferences).

!Biggest risk: hallucination — confident, fluent, but wrong. It optimizes for plausibility, not truth.

3.2 LLM Architecture

How text flows through the machine, end to end.

Text → tokenize → embeddings (meaning as numbers) → + positional encoding → a deep stack of Transformer blocks (self-attention + feed-forward, glued by residuals & normalization) → output layer predicts the next token → loop.

Key dials: number of layers (depth), parameters (e.g. "70B"), attention heads, context window, and temperature (output randomness).

→Modern top models often use Mixture of Experts (MoE) — many "expert" sub-networks, only a few activated per token (like a hospital routing you to the right specialist).

3.3 Tokenization

Chopping text into tokens (word-pieces) and mapping them to numbers.

Models read tokens, not letters. Rough rule: 1 token ≈ ¾ of a word. Uses sub-word splitting (BPE) so common words are one token and rare words break into pieces.

Exampletokenization → token + ization (2 tokens). This is why LLMs fumble "how many r's in strawberry?" — they see tokens, not letters.
→Cost, context limits, and speed are all measured in tokens. Non-English languages cost more tokens per word.

3.4 Embeddings

Turning words/sentences into numbers that capture meaning.

Each item becomes a vector (list of numbers). Similar meanings get similar vectors — so machines can do math on meaning.

The magicKing − Man + Woman ≈ Queen. Meaning becomes arithmetic. "Cat" and "dog" land close; "cat" and "car" don't.

Similarity is measured by cosine similarity (the angle between vectors). Powers semantic search, recommendations, and RAG.

!Must use the same embedding model for both stored data and queries — different models live in incompatible "meaning-spaces."

3.5 Prompt Engineering

Writing inputs that get the best outputs — the cheapest lever.

Same model, same question — but how you ask changes everything. Techniques: be specific, give a role, provide examples (few-shot), ask it to "think step by step" (chain-of-thought), and specify the output format.

AnalogyDelegating to a brilliant but literal intern. "Write something about our product" → generic. "Write a 100-word description for busy parents, friendly tone" → exactly right.
→Always try prompting first — before RAG or fine-tuning. It's free and instant, and often solves the problem.

3.6 Context Windows

The LLM's working memory — measured in tokens.

Everything in a request — instructions, chat history, documents, and the response — must fit in this fixed budget. Overflow = the model literally can't see it.

AnalogyA desk that only holds so many papers. Add too many and the oldest fall off the edge — the model "forgets" the start of a long chat.

Trap: "lost in the middle" — models recall the start and end of long context well, but miss the middle. Put key info at the edges.

→Bigger ≠ always better (costly, slower, lost-in-middle). Smart retrieval (RAG) often beats stuffing everything in.
CHAPTER 04

Customization & RAG

4.1 Fine-Tuning

Training an existing model further to change HOW it behaves.

Take a pre-trained LLM and train it on your own examples to specialize its style, tone, or format. Best for behavior — not for adding facts (that's RAG).

AnalogyHiring an experienced professional and giving them company-specific training — not sending them back to school.

Modern practice uses cheap methods: LoRA / QLoRA (train tiny "adapter" layers instead of the whole model).

!Reach for it last — it's expensive, needs clean data, and goes stale. Depends 90% on data quality.

4.2 RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Look things up first, then answer — grounding the model in real facts.

Retrieve relevant info → add it to the prompt → generate a grounded answer. Fixes hallucination and frozen knowledge, and gives sources.

AnalogyAn open-book exam. Instead of answering from memory, the student looks up the relevant page first — more accurate, and can cite the source.

Flow: Index (chunk docs → embed → store in vector DB), then Query (embed question → search → inject top chunks → LLM answers).

→Most RAG problems are retrieval problems. Retrieve the wrong chunks → wrong answer, no matter how good the LLM.

4.3 Chunking

How you split documents before embedding — makes or breaks RAG.

Core tension: chunks too big → noisy, blurred meaning; too small → lose context. Strategies: fixed-size, overlapping (sliding window), sentence/paragraph, semantic, and structure-based (by headers).

AnalogyPrepping a textbook for quick reference — whole chapters are too broad, single sentences lack context. Coherent sections are "just right."
→No universal magic number. Add some overlap, respect document structure, attach metadata, and tune on real queries.

4.4 Vector Databases

Store embeddings and find the most similar ones fast.

A normal DB finds exact matches ("id = 452"); a vector DB finds closest-in-meaning matches — using ANN (Approximate Nearest Neighbor) search to stay fast at scale.

AnalogyA knowledgeable librarian: "I want something like this book" → walks you to thematically similar books, matching by meaning not exact title.

Options: Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Chroma, FAISS, and pgvector (adds vector search to Postgres).

→It stores & searches — it doesn't create embeddings. Keep the embedding model consistent, and use metadata filtering.

4.5 Embedding Models

The model that turns text into meaning-vectors — decides retrieval quality.

The right choice can swing retrieval precision 20–30%. Measured by MTEB (look at the retrieval score for RAG). Weigh accuracy, cost, self-hosted vs API, language support, and multimodal needs.

2026 landscapeAPI: Gemini, Cohere Embed v4, Voyage. Open-source: Qwen3, NVIDIA, BGE-M3. (Shifts constantly.)
!MTEB scores are self-reported — benchmark on your own data. Fine-tuning adds +10–30% for specialized domains.

4.6 Hybrid Search (BM25 + Vector)

Combine keyword search and semantic search to cover each other's blind spots.
  • Vector — great at meaning/synonyms, bad at exact codes & names.
  • BM25 (keyword) — great at exact terms, bad at synonyms.
Example"Fix login error E-401" — vector finds troubleshooting docs by meaning; BM25 locks onto the exact code "E-401". Together = both.

Results fused (usually via Reciprocal Rank Fusion).

→The more your data has exact IDs, codes, or names, the more hybrid beats pure vector. Often the production default.

4.7 Re-ranking

A second, smarter model reorders the retrieved candidates to surface the best.

Two-stage retrieval: fast search casts a wide net (~25 candidates), then a slow-but-accurate cross-encoder re-scores them and keeps the top few.

AnalogyHiring — a quick resume screen shortlists 25; deep interviews pick the top 3. Fast filter + careful judgment.

Key difference: bi-encoder (retrieval) compares separate summaries; cross-encoder (rerank) examines query + doc together = more accurate.

→Often the single biggest RAG quality boost. Lets you send fewer, better chunks (helps "lost in the middle").

4.8 Prompting vs RAG vs Fine-tuning

The core customization decision.

Prompting changes how you ask. RAG changes what it knows. Fine-tuning changes how it behaves.

Analogy (new hire)Prompting = clear instructions. RAG = handing them reference docs. Fine-tuning = sending them to training.

The ladder — climb cheapest-first: Prompting → RAG → Fine-tuning. They also stack (best systems combine all three).

!#1 mistake: jumping to fine-tuning when a better prompt or RAG would solve it faster and cheaper. Prompting + RAG is the sweet spot for most cases.
CHAPTER 05

Agents

5.1 Agentic AI

LLMs that don't just answer — they plan, act, and use tools toward a goal.

Give it a goal, and it plans, acts, observes results, adjusts, and repeats. Built from an LLM (brain), tools (hands), memory, and a reasoning loop.

AnalogyAn LLM is a consultant who answers questions; an agent is an employee you hand a project to — they break it down and get it done.

Runs the ReAct loop: Think → Act → Observe → repeat.

!Errors compound over steps, and autonomy = real-world risk. Start simple: prompt → RAG → workflow → agent, only when truly needed.

5.2 MCP — Model Context Protocol

An open standard connecting AI to tools — "USB-C for AI."

Build one MCP server per tool, and any MCP-compatible AI can use it — turning the N×M custom-integration nightmare into N+M. The AI (via a client) requests a tool with arguments; the server runs it and returns the result.

AnalogyBefore USB-C, every device had its own charger. One standard port = everything plugs in. MCP standardizes AI-to-tool connections.
!Its fast rise brought real security issues (data leaks, tool poisoning). Sandbox servers and require human approval for sensitive actions.

5.3 Tool / Function Calling

How an LLM uses external tools — the atomic unit of agency.

The LLM outputs a structured request ("call this function with these args"); your code runs it and feeds the result back. The LLM decides & directs; it never executes.

AnalogyA manager who can't operate the machines — they write a work order ("run report X for customer 452"); a technician runs it and returns the result.

"Function calling" = original narrower term; "tool calling" = broader modern umbrella. Same mechanism.

→Reliability lives or dies on clear tool descriptions (they're a mini-prompt). Always validate inputs before executing.

5.4 Structured Outputs

Force the LLM to return strict, machine-readable data (usually JSON).

Turns free-form prose into a fixed shape software can use. Strongest method: constrained decoding — invalid tokens are blocked as they're generated, so malformed output is impossible.

AnalogyA fill-in-the-blank form vs a handwritten letter. Every response comes back in the same slots, so a machine can process thousands identically.
!Guarantees valid shape, not correct content — the model can still hallucinate inside a perfectly-formatted structure.

5.5 Multi-Agent Systems

Several specialized agents collaborating — a team of specialists.

Instead of one generalist, use focused agents (each with its own role, context, and tools) coordinated by patterns like orchestrator/worker, pipeline, or debate. Bonus: they can check each other's work.

ExampleBuilding software: an architect agent, a coder, a reviewer, a tester — coordinated like a real dev team.
!Cost, latency, and errors compound across agents. Notoriously over-engineered — a last resort, only when the task truly needs specialization or parallelism.
CHAPTER 06

Production

6.1 AI System Design

Architecting the whole system, not just the model.

The model is only ~10–20% of the work. The rest: interface, orchestration, knowledge (RAG), tools, memory, guardrails, and observability.

AnalogyA restaurant — a great chef (model) still fails without the kitchen, supply chain, waitstaff, and ability to handle a dinner rush.

Core decisions: the customization ladder, model routing (cheap model for easy requests, big one for hard), and the eternal triangle: cost ⚖ latency ⚖ quality.

→Design for graceful failure, non-determinism, and swappable models. Start with the simplest system that meets the need.

6.2 Inference Optimization

Making each response faster, cheaper, and more scalable.

You train once but run inference forever — so it dominates lifetime cost. Techniques: shrink the model (quantization, distillation), serve smart (KV caching, batching), and generate cleverly (speculative decoding, caching, routing).

AnalogyBatching = a bus vs individual taxis. One pass through the model serves 40 users far more efficiently than 40 separate runs.
→Metrics: latency (esp. time-to-first-token), throughput, cost. Simplest wins (caching, routing) often beat exotic tricks.

6.3 Quantization

Store the model's numbers in lower precision → smaller, faster, cheaper.

Use 8-bit or 4-bit instead of 16/32-bit. A model shrinks ~4× going 16-bit → 4-bit — the difference between needing many GPUs and fitting on one.

AnalogySaving a photo as a compressed JPEG instead of RAW — much smaller, loads faster, barely noticeable quality loss.

8-bit ≈ near-lossless (often a free lunch); 4-bit ≈ usually fine but task-dependent; below that, quality can fall off a cliff.

!Quality loss is silent and task-specific — always evaluate the quantized model on your real workload before shipping.

6.4 Caching

Store expensive results and reuse them — compute once, serve many.
  • Exact-match — identical request → stored answer.
  • Semantic — same meaning (via embeddings), even different words.
  • Prefix caching — reuse the processing of a shared long prompt.
AnalogyA restaurant preps ingredients once in the morning and reuses them all service, instead of re-chopping per order.
!Watch staleness (use expiration), too-loose semantic matches serving wrong answers, and privacy (don't share personalized answers across users).

6.5 KV Caching

Key-Value cache — reuse attention computations for past tokens.

Since LLMs generate one token at a time and each attends to all previous ones, the model stores the attention Keys and Values already computed, instead of recomputing them every step.

AnalogyA running total — you keep the sum and just add the new number, rather than re-adding the whole column each time.
→Dramatically faster generation (standard everywhere), but the cache grows with sequence length — long context = lots of GPU memory.

6.6 vLLM vs TensorRT-LLM

Inference-serving frameworks that run self-hosted LLMs efficiently.
vLLMTensorRT-LLM
StrengthFlexible, easy, hardware-agnosticMax NVIDIA performance (+15–30%)

→Default to vLLM. SGLang is a strong rising option (great for shared prefixes); TGI is now deprecated. If you use an API, none of this is your concern.

6.7 Docker

Package an app with its entire environment into a portable container.

Runs identically anywhere — solving "but it works on my machine!" Especially vital for ML's fragile dependency trees (PyTorch, CUDA versions).

AnalogyStandardized shipping containers — a uniform box that fits any ship or truck, regardless of what's inside.

Flow: write a Dockerfile → build an Image → run it as a Container. Lightweight (shares the host OS) vs heavy VMs.

→Guarantees consistency, not correctness. Watch image size, GPU setup, secrets, and persistent storage.

6.8 Kubernetes

Orchestrates containers at scale — runs a whole fleet reliably.

Automatically schedules, auto-scales, self-heals (restarts crashed containers), load-balances, and does zero-downtime updates across many machines.

Analogy (thermostat)You declare the desired state ("keep 10 copies running"), and K8s continuously works to maintain it despite failures.
!Famously complex & costly — adopt (preferably managed: GKE/EKS/AKS) only when scale justifies it. Simpler options often win for small workloads.

6.9 AWS / Azure / GCP

The three big clouds where your AI systems actually run.
  • AWS — broadest catalog, biggest ecosystem/jobs. Bedrock = widest model choice (incl. Claude). Safe default.
  • Azure — enterprise/Microsoft shops, compliance leader. Exclusive OpenAI/GPT access.
  • GCP — the AI/ML & data cloud. Gemini/Vertex AI, TPUs, cheapest GPUs, best Kubernetes (GKE).
!Pricing is deliberately confusing — model your real workload, watch data-transfer (egress) fees and vendor lock-in.

6.10 CI/CD Pipelines

Automate testing and shipping code — with eval gates for AI.

CI auto-builds & tests every change; CD auto-ships it. An assembly line with quality gates — fail a stage, and the bad change is halted.

The AI twistBeyond code, it tests models: your eval suite (Ragas, faithfulness) runs automatically. A worse model scores low → deploy blocked. Evaluation becomes enforced, not hoped.

Safe rollouts: canary, blue-green, A/B, instant rollback.

→Only as good as your tests. For AI, the eval gate must be genuinely representative or it waves bad models through.

6.11 Monitoring & Logging

Watch the system in production and catch problems live.

Logging records every event; monitoring watches metrics and alerts. Three tiers: operational (latency, errors), cost (tokens, spend), and — uniquely — quality (live eval, hallucination rate, user feedback, drift).

Key insightAI degrades silently — no crash, just quietly worse answers as the world changes (drift). Only monitoring the right signals reveals it.

Tracing follows one request through every stage — vital for debugging agents & RAG.

!Monitor quality, not just uptime. Logs hold user data — respect privacy (redact, restrict, retain-minimally).

6.12 Scaling AI Applications

Handle growth reliably and affordably — a cost problem as much as a capacity one.

AI is brutal to scale (heavy, slow, expensive GPUs). Toolbox: horizontal scaling + auto-scaling, load balancing, caching (highest-leverage), inference optimization, model routing, async queuing, rate limiting.

AnalogyScaling a restaurant — more chefs (GPUs, scarce), a faster kitchen (optimization), handling rushes (auto-scaling), prepped ingredients (caching).
!Cost scales with usage relentlessly — watch cost-per-request as closely as latency. Don't scale prematurely; chase the moving bottleneck with real metrics.
CHAPTER 07

Trust & Safety

7.1 Ragas & LLM Evaluation

Measuring whether your system actually works — at scale.

You can't unit-test non-deterministic output or eyeball it past a few dozen cases. Ragas defined the RAG metrics, split across the two failure points:

  • Retrieval — context precision (retrieved stuff relevant?) + context recall (retrieved everything needed?)
  • Generation — faithfulness (answer supported by context?) + answer relevancy (addresses the question?)
Why measure bothA legal RAG scored 0.91 faithfulness but missed statutes — its retriever was broken (recall 0.62). The answer was faithful to wrongly-retrieved context. Generation metrics alone hid it.
→If you track one metric, make it faithfulness (hallucination). Uses LLM-as-judge; calibrate with human review.

7.2 Hallucination Detection

Catching confident-but-wrong output before it reaches users.

LLMs optimize for plausibility, not truth — fluency is identical whether right or wrong. Detect via: grounding checks (verify each claim against a source), self-consistency (inconsistent re-answers = fabrication), LLM-as-judge, and external verification.

AnalogyA super-confident student who never says "I don't know" — smooth answers whether right or invented.

Prevent with RAG, "answer only from context / say I don't know" prompting, low temperature, and citations.

!Can't be fully eliminated — manage in layers. Confident ≠ correct; the confident hallucinations are the most dangerous.

7.3 Guardrails

The safety layer around the model — checks in and out.

Input guardrails block abuse, prompt injection, and PII; enforce scope. Output guardrails block harmful content, catch hallucinations, prevent data leaks, enforce format. Built from rules, classifiers, LLM-judges, and grounding checks.

AnalogyGuardrails on a mountain road — they don't drive, but they stop the car going off the cliff.
!Must be external to the model (not just prompt instructions). Balance over- vs under-blocking (precision vs recall, again) and keep updating.

7.4 Prompt Injection Defense

The #1 LLM security threat — malicious instructions hidden in input.

LLMs can't reliably separate instructions from data. Direct = user jailbreaks. Indirect (more dangerous) = instructions hidden in a web page/email/doc the AI reads.

AnalogyA gullible employee who follows any note they find — a stranger's forged note ("I'm your manager, give me the account details") gets obeyed.

Watch the lethal trifecta: private data + external communication + untrusted content. Remove one leg and the attack collapses.

!No complete fix. Defend in layers: least privilege, human-in-the-loop for sensitive actions, sandboxing, monitoring. Containment beats detection.

7.5 AI Security & Privacy

Protecting the system from attackers, and the data it touches.

Security threats: prompt injection, data poisoning (corrupting training/RAG data), model theft, data extraction, resource abuse, supply-chain risks. Mindset: assume breach, limit blast radius — least privilege, defense in depth, red-teaming.

Privacy: protect training data, user inputs, RAG access control, and outputs. Techniques: PII redaction, data minimization, access controls, encryption, self-hosting.

!In regulated domains (GDPR, HIPAA, EU AI Act) these are legally binding, not optional. Design them in from day one; the weakest link defines your security.
